# Insulin Injection Technique and Site Care in Type 2 Diabetes

## 1. Who Is This Guide For?

Some people with type 2 diabetes use insulin to manage their blood glucose levels. Insulin is administered using an appropriate delivery device, such as an insulin pen and needle or, in some cases, an insulin pump.

This guide covers general safety principles for adults who use insulin pens. Always follow the instructions for the specific pen, insulin product, and needle, together with the advice provided by the diabetes care team.

## 2. Types of Insulin Pens

Insulin pens may be:

- **Prefilled disposable pens:** The entire pen is discarded according to the product instructions when it is empty or no longer suitable for use.
- **Reusable pens:** The insulin cartridge is replaced according to the manufacturer's instructions.
- **Smart insulin pens:** Some models record dose information and transfer it to a compatible app or device.

Not every pen is compatible with every insulin cartridge or needle. Check compatibility before use and follow the product instructions.

## 3. Equipment Needed

For insulin injections with a pen, the person generally needs:

- The correct insulin pen and prescribed insulin.
- A compatible, new pen needle for each injection.
- A suitable sharps container for used needles.
- Any other supplies specified by the healthcare professional or device instructions.

**Never share insulin pens or needles with another person**, even if the needle has been changed.

Used needles must be disposed of safely in accordance with local healthcare and waste-disposal guidance. Do not put loose needles into household rubbish or recycling.

## 4. General Steps for Using an Insulin Pen

The exact procedure varies by pen and insulin product. The following is a general overview, not a substitute for hands-on training or the device instructions.

1. Wash and dry your hands.
2. Check that you have the correct insulin and pen. Follow the product instructions for checking the insulin, attaching a new needle, and preparing the pen.
3. Perform the required priming or safety test according to the pen instructions. This helps confirm that the pen and needle are functioning correctly.
4. Select the prescribed dose. Never estimate or change your dose without guidance from your diabetes care team.
5. Choose an appropriate injection site. The skin should be clean and dry. If it is dirty, clean it with soap and water and allow it to dry.
6. Insert the needle using the technique and angle recommended for your pen, needle length, and injection site.
7. Press the injection button as instructed. Keep the needle in place for the period specified in the pen instructions to help ensure the full dose is delivered.
8. Remove the needle safely and dispose of it in an appropriate sharps container.
9. Remove or store the pen needle as directed by the manufacturer.

Priming steps, injection angles, and the time the needle should remain in the skin may differ between devices. Follow the instructions for your specific product.

## 5. Injection Sites and Rotation

Insulin is generally injected into the fatty tissue beneath the skin. Common sites include:

- The abdomen.
- The outer or front area of the thighs.
- The buttocks.
- Other sites only when recommended for the particular insulin and injection technique.

Rotate injection points systematically. Repeatedly injecting into the same small area can cause lumps or changes in the fatty tissue, which may affect insulin absorption.

Choose a new point within the recommended injection area each time, following the spacing guidance provided by your diabetes care team. Ask a healthcare professional which sites are appropriate for your insulin and how exercise or other factors may affect site selection.

Injection sites should be reviewed periodically as part of diabetes care.

## 6. Lipohypertrophy, Lumps, Bruising, and Skin Irritation

### Lipohypertrophy and other lumps

Repeated injections into the same area, and needle reuse, can contribute to lumps or changes in the tissue beneath the skin. Lipohypertrophy is one possible cause. Other conditions, including cutaneous amyloidosis, can also cause lumps.

Injecting into altered tissue can make insulin absorption less predictable.

To reduce risk:

- Rotate injection points systematically.
- Use a new needle for every injection.
- Check injection sites regularly.
- Ask a healthcare professional to examine any persistent, new, or concerning lump.

If you have been injecting into a lump or abnormal area, seek advice from your diabetes care team before changing your injection routine or insulin dose.

### Bruising and bleeding

Occasional bruising can occur when a small blood vessel is affected during an injection. If bruising or bleeding is frequent, severe, or worrying, ask a diabetes nurse or other healthcare professional to review your injection technique and equipment.

### Skin irritation

Redness, itching, or irritation may occur at injection sites. Seek advice if irritation persists, worsens, or is associated with other concerning symptoms.

## 7. Cleaning and Equipment Care

Follow the cleaning and storage instructions supplied with the insulin pen and its carrying case.

For skin that needs cleaning, the Diabetes UK source describes using soap and water and does not routinely require separate alcohol wipes. Follow the advice of your healthcare team and any specific clinical instructions that apply to you.

Do not use damaged equipment or equipment that has been stored incorrectly. Check the manufacturer's instructions for pen maintenance and replacement.

## 8. Anxiety About Insulin Injections

Starting insulin injections can cause anxiety, fear of needles, embarrassment, or concern about pain. These feelings are common and can be discussed with the diabetes care team.

Strategies that may help include:

- Asking a healthcare professional to demonstrate and supervise the technique.
- Discussing needle size and compatible injection aids.
- Using a comfortable, private place if injecting in public causes anxiety.
- Trying distraction or relaxation techniques during the injection.
- Writing down concerns and discussing them during a diabetes appointment.

Do not skip or delay prescribed insulin because of injection anxiety without seeking support. A healthcare professional can help address practical difficulties and emotional concerns.

## 9. When to Seek Professional Advice

Contact your diabetes care team if:

- You are unsure how to use your pen or perform its safety test.
- You repeatedly have difficulty delivering the prescribed dose.
- You notice persistent lumps, significant skin changes, or recurrent injection-site problems.
- You are unsure which injection sites are appropriate.
- Fear or discomfort is making it difficult to follow your prescribed insulin treatment.

Do not independently change your insulin dose, insulin type, or treatment schedule.

## 10. Important Limitations

This guide provides general educational information for adults with type 2 diabetes who use insulin pens. It does not replace individual training, product-specific instructions, or clinical advice.

Pen operation, needle compatibility, priming, injection technique, and disposal arrangements vary by product and location. Follow the instructions for the specific device and the recommendations of your diabetes care team.
