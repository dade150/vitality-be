# Travelling with Diabetes

## 1. Overview

Living with diabetes should not prevent a person from travelling at home or abroad. However, travel can disrupt normal routines and affect diabetes management because of changes in meal timing, physical activity, temperature, time zones, and access to medicines or healthcare.

Planning ahead can help reduce avoidable problems and make it easier to manage diabetes during a trip.

The source recommends discussing travel arrangements with the diabetes healthcare team, carrying sufficient medicines and equipment, obtaining appropriate documentation, and checking the requirements of airlines and destination countries.

## 2. Planning medication and supplies

### Additional supplies

Diabetes UK recommends considering supplies of approximately **two to three times the amount of medication and equipment normally needed**. The purpose is to provide a reserve if the trip is extended or disrupted.

Before departure:

- Ask the healthcare team for prescriptions covering additional medication and supplies.
- Bring spare equipment and a backup method of managing diabetes if a device fails.
- Ask how to obtain replacement insulin or other diabetes medicines at the destination.
- Check whether the insulin product used at home is available in the destination country.
- Carry a recent prescription.
- Discuss any planned changes in insulin timing or doses before travelling across time zones.

The two-to-three-times quantity is the recommendation reported by this source. The amount required in practice should be planned with the healthcare team and should account for the length and nature of the trip.

### Medical letter

Some airlines or other travel operators may request a letter from the person's GP or diabetes healthcare team confirming the diagnosis and the need to carry diabetes medicines and equipment.

The letter may include:

- The person's diabetes diagnosis and treatment.
- Medicines and relevant medical supplies.
- Insulin, injection equipment, or diabetes technology carried during travel.
- An explanation of any medically necessary liquids exceeding the usual airport liquid-container limit.

Check the airline's current requirements before departure. A letter may also be useful if medical assistance is needed during the trip.

### Airline and destination requirements

Before travel:

- Check the airline's or travel operator's guidance for passengers with diabetes.
- Ask whether forms or advance notification are required, particularly for insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitors (CGMs).
- Review health and medication information for the destination country.
- Check restrictions on importing prescribed medicines.

The source refers UK travelers to the UK government's foreign travel advice pages for destination-specific information.

## 3. Travel insurance and access to care

Travel insurance should be checked carefully before departure.

- Confirm that the policy explicitly covers pre-existing conditions, including diabetes.
- Check cover for emergency treatment, medical assistance, and medical evacuation or repatriation.
- Review exclusions, limits, and the procedure for contacting the insurer during an emergency.
- Identify local healthcare services and possible sources of replacement medicines at the destination.

The source notes that access to publicly funded healthcare in some destinations does not necessarily cover every cost, such as emergency repatriation. Coverage depends on the destination, eligibility, and the applicable arrangements.

## 4. What to pack in hand luggage

Diabetes medicines and essential equipment should remain accessible during travel. The source recommends carrying these items in hand luggage rather than checked baggage.

### Hand-luggage checklist

| Item | Purpose |
|---|---|
| All necessary diabetes medicines | Maintain treatment during the journey and in case of delays. |
| Extra medication and device supplies | Provide a reserve if the trip is extended or equipment fails. |
| Blood glucose meter and testing supplies, if used | Allow blood glucose checks when needed. |
| CGM or flash glucose monitoring supplies, if used | Support continued monitoring and replacement of essential components. |
| Diabetes identity card or medical identification bracelet | Help others identify the medical condition in an emergency. |
| Medical letter | Explain the need to carry medicines, devices, and relevant supplies. |
| Recent prescription | Support access to replacement medication if necessary. |
| Hypoglycemia treatment, if relevant | Provide a suitable treatment option if blood glucose becomes low. |
| Additional snacks | Help manage delays or unexpected changes in meal timing. |

The source suggests separating supplies between bags or, where appropriate, sharing some supplies with a travelling companion to reduce the risk of losing everything if one bag goes missing.

### Carrying insulin on an aircraft

The source advises carrying insulin in hand luggage because checked baggage may be exposed to unsuitable temperatures and may be lost or delayed.

Insulin should be stored according to the product's instructions. Avoid excessive heat and freezing, and use an appropriate cooling solution if required.

### Airport liquid restrictions

The source describes the usual UK airport rule limiting liquid containers in hand luggage to 100 mL, while noting that medically necessary liquids may require special arrangements.

Because airport screening rules and exemptions can change, check the current requirements of the departure airport, transit airports, and airline before travelling. Carry medical documentation when appropriate.

## 5. Flying with an insulin pump, CGM, or flash glucose monitor

### Before departure

- Check the device manufacturer's current guidance on air travel and security screening.
- Contact the airline well in advance to determine whether notification or forms are required.
- Carry medical documentation explaining the need for the device.
- Discuss a backup plan with the diabetes healthcare team in case a pump or monitoring device fails or must temporarily be removed.

### Using devices during a flight

The source states that insulin pumps, CGMs, and flash glucose monitoring systems can generally be used during air travel, subject to device-specific instructions.

It also warns that changes in cabin pressure may affect insulin delivery from some pumps. More frequent monitoring may therefore be appropriate during a flight, particularly around take-off and landing.

Device-specific recommendations take precedence. Follow the manufacturer's instructions and the airline's current requirements.

### Airport security screening

The source distinguishes between different screening methods and warns that some medical devices may be unsuitable for exposure to particular scanners.

| Screening method | Guidance reported by the source |
|---|---|
| Walk-through metal detector | Many CGM sensors and insulin pumps can pass through these detectors. Check the instructions for the particular device. |
| X-ray baggage scanner | The source advises that many diabetes devices and spare sensors or equipment should not be exposed to baggage X-ray scanners. |
| Full-body scanner | The source advises checking device-specific restrictions and requesting an alternative screening method, such as a pat-down, when necessary. |

Do not assume that all devices have the same screening restrictions. Confirm the permitted screening methods with the device manufacturer and the relevant airport or airline.

The source recommends carrying a medical device awareness card, where available, together with a medical letter. It states that travelers should be offered an alternative screening method when their device cannot undergo a particular screening procedure.

### Medical device awareness card

The source refers to a medical device awareness card from the UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA). Availability and current guidance should be checked directly with the CAA.

## 6. Managing insulin across time zones

Long-haul travel across time zones may require changes to insulin timing or doses.

The source describes the following general considerations:

- Eastward travel shortens the local day and may require a temporary reduction in insulin.
- Westward travel lengthens the local day and may increase insulin requirements.

These are general considerations, not dosing instructions. The actual adjustment depends on the individual's treatment regimen, itinerary, meals, and clinical circumstances.

Before departure:

- Discuss the flight itinerary with the diabetes healthcare team.
- Provide the departure time, flight duration, destination, and local arrival time.
- Obtain a specific plan for insulin injections or pump settings.
- Clarify when to use the home time zone and when to change to local time.

Do not calculate or change doses solely from the direction of travel without an individualized plan.

### Diabetes tablets and time zones

The source states that people taking diabetes tablets may not usually experience major difficulties with time-zone changes, but occasional adjustments may be needed when the travel day becomes longer or shorter.

Any decision to add, delay, or omit a dose should be discussed with the healthcare team before departure. The source does not provide a universal adjustment schedule.

### Insulin pumps, CGMs, and readers

When crossing time zones:

- Some insulin pumps may require a manual change to the time or date to maintain the intended basal insulin schedule.
- Confirm which settings should be changed and when with the healthcare team and device manufacturer.
- Smartphones may update their time automatically, but this should not be assumed to update every connected device.
- The source notes that some flash glucose readers may require a manual time adjustment for accurate reports.

Check the instructions for the specific pump, reader, sensor, and associated application.

## 7. Storing insulin in hot and cold climates

### Hot climates

High temperatures can damage insulin and reduce its effectiveness.

The source advises travelers to:

- Avoid exposing insulin to excessive heat or direct sunlight.
- Use a suitable cool bag or appropriate refrigerator storage when necessary.
- Avoid direct contact between insulin and frozen cooling packs.
- Check the insulin's appearance and product-specific instructions before use.

The source describes heat-damaged insulin as potentially changing appearance, including clear insulin becoming cloudy or developing a brownish colour. Appearance alone cannot reliably establish whether insulin is safe or effective. If insulin may have been exposed to unsuitable temperatures, consult the product instructions or a pharmacist and replace it when advised.

### Cold climates

Insulin that has frozen must not be used, according to the source.

For travel in very cold conditions:

- Protect insulin from freezing.
- Follow the product's storage instructions.
- Consider keeping it in an insulated pouch close to the body when appropriate, while avoiding overheating.
- Check the temperature limits specified for the blood glucose meter and other equipment.
- Keep meters and supplies within their specified operating temperature range.

### Summary of temperature precautions

| Situation | Main precaution |
|---|---|
| Hot climate | Protect insulin and devices from excessive heat and direct sunlight. |
| Cool bag with frozen packs | Prevent direct contact with frozen packs and avoid freezing insulin. |
| Very cold climate | Protect insulin from freezing and follow product-specific storage instructions. |
| Blood glucose meter in extreme temperatures | Check the manufacturer's operating-temperature limits because readings may be affected. |
| Suspected temperature damage | Consult the product instructions or a pharmacist; do not rely on appearance alone. |

## 8. Food and blood glucose management during flights

Airline meal schedules can help travelers plan meals and treatment.

The source recommends:

- Checking meal times with the airline when possible.
- Considering whether the standard meal provides enough carbohydrate for the person's treatment needs.
- Carrying suitable snacks for delays and for periods between meals.
- Monitoring blood glucose more frequently when necessary, particularly for people using insulin or medicines that can cause hypoglycemia.
- Following the individualized diabetes care plan if meal timing changes.

Snacks and meal choices should be adapted to the person's treatment and clinical needs. The source does not establish a universal requirement for a bedtime snack or a specific carbohydrate amount.

## 9. Managing diabetes in hot climates

### Sun protection and foot care

The source advises protecting the skin and feet during travel in hot climates.

- Wear clothing that protects the skin from excessive sun exposure.
- Use appropriate sunscreen and sunglasses with suitable UV protection.
- Pay particular attention to the hands, neck, and other exposed areas.
- Protect the feet from hot sand and surfaces.
- Wear well-fitting footwear, including at the beach.
- People with neuropathy or reduced foot sensation should take extra care because burns or injuries may not be noticed promptly.

### Heat, activity, and blood glucose

Hot weather and changes in activity may affect diabetes management.

The source notes that:

- Sunbathing or being less active than usual may be associated with higher blood glucose in some circumstances.
- Insulin absorption from an injection site may be faster in hot weather, potentially increasing hypoglycemia risk.
- Extreme temperatures may affect the accuracy of some blood glucose meters.

Monitor blood glucose as appropriate, follow the person's established treatment plan, and seek clinical advice if results are unexpected or difficult to manage.

## 10. Managing diabetes in cold climates

Cold conditions can affect insulin absorption, energy use, blood glucose monitoring, and foot health.

The source describes the possibility of insulin absorption initially slowing in cold conditions and changing as the body warms up. Shivering and increased energy use to maintain body temperature may also affect blood glucose.

Precautions include:

- Wearing layers so clothing can be adjusted as conditions change.
- Monitoring blood glucose more often when clinically appropriate.
- Carrying suitable treatment for hypoglycemia when relevant.
- Checking the meter manufacturer's operating-temperature limits.
- Protecting insulin from freezing.
- Checking the feet regularly, especially when neuropathy or poor circulation is present.
- Taking spare socks and appropriate footwear to help prevent cold injury.

Hypoglycemia in cold conditions can be particularly dangerous because it may impair the body's ability to maintain warmth. Follow the individualized diabetes care plan and seek medical help if symptoms are severe or cannot be managed safely.

## 11. Maintaining routines and managing illness

Travel may involve unfamiliar foods, altered activity, alcohol consumption, different meal schedules, and disrupted sleep. These changes can affect blood glucose.

The source recommends continuing regular diabetes-management routines as far as practical and checking blood glucose when needed.

If the traveler develops a gastrointestinal illness or becomes unable to eat or drink:

- Follow the person's established sick-day plan.
- Seek advice from the diabetes healthcare team or an appropriate medical service when needed.
- Do not assume that all diabetes medicines should be continued or stopped in the same way during illness.
- Seek urgent medical attention if the condition worsens or warning signs develop.

For COVID-19 or other infectious illnesses during travel, follow applicable local public-health guidance.

### Blood glucose units

Blood glucose may be reported in different units in different countries, commonly mmol/L or mg/dL.

Travelers should know which units their meter uses and how to interpret results reported by local healthcare services. Use a reliable conversion reference when necessary and avoid confusing the two units.

## 12. Source and limitations

This document is a structured summary of Diabetes UK's patient guidance *Travelling with diabetes*. Repeated material has been consolidated into topic-based sections to support information retrieval.

Some recommendations in the source are specific to the United Kingdom, including references to the UK Civil Aviation Authority, GP letters, and UK travel advice. Airport security rules, medication import restrictions, device compatibility, and insurance coverage vary by destination and may change over time.

The document does not provide individualized medication instructions. Insulin dose changes, adjustments to tablet schedules, sick-day management, and device settings should be planned with the person's healthcare team.
