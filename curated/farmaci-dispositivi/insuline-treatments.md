# Medicines and Other Treatments for Type 2 Diabetes

## Overview

Type 2 diabetes is managed through an individualized plan that may include healthy eating, physical activity, weight management when appropriate, sleep, stress management, and medication.

Some people can initially manage their blood glucose through lifestyle changes, while many need medication as well. Over time, treatment may need to change or include more than one medicine to maintain blood glucose within an individualized target range.

The choice of treatment depends on several factors, including:
- Blood glucose management needs.
- Other health conditions.
- Potential side effects.
- Medication costs, insurance coverage, and access.
- Lifestyle and personal preferences.

A healthcare professional can help determine which treatment is appropriate. Patients should not start, stop, or change medication doses without discussing the change with their healthcare team.

## Medicines used to treat type 2 diabetes

Several types of medicines are available for type 2 diabetes. They lower blood glucose through different mechanisms, and more than one medicine may be needed.

### Oral medicines

Many medicines for type 2 diabetes are taken by mouth.

Metformin is one commonly used treatment. It reduces the amount of glucose produced by the liver and helps the body use insulin more effectively. It is available as tablets and liquid.

Other oral medicines work through different mechanisms. Combining medicines with complementary effects may improve blood glucose management compared with using a single medicine.

The most appropriate medication depends on the individual's clinical situation and treatment goals. This guide does not provide a complete list of medication classes or recommendations for choosing between them.

### Injectable medicines other than insulin

Some injectable medicines used in type 2 diabetes help control the rise in blood glucose after meals.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are one such group. They can help regulate blood glucose and may reduce appetite and support weight loss.

GLP-1 receptor agonists are not substitutes for insulin when insulin is medically necessary. Whether one of these medicines is appropriate depends on the person's treatment needs and clinical circumstances.

### Insulin treatment

Some people with type 2 diabetes need insulin, either as part of their ongoing treatment or during particular circumstances, such as hospitalization or pregnancy.

Needing insulin does not mean that a person has failed to manage their diabetes. Treatment requirements can change over time as the body's insulin needs and ability to produce or use insulin change.

Insulin type, timing, and delivery method must be selected and adjusted with the prescribing healthcare professional.

## Understanding insulin types

Insulin preparations differ in how quickly they begin to work, when they have their strongest effect, and how long their effects last.

The NIDDK source describes the following broad categories:

| Insulin type | Onset | Peak | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rapid-acting or ultra-rapid-acting | About 15 minutes | About 1 hour | About 2–4 hours for rapid-acting; 5–7 hours for ultra-rapid-acting |
| Inhaled rapid-acting insulin | About 10–15 minutes | About 30 minutes | About 3 hours |
| Regular or short-acting | About 30 minutes | About 2–3 hours | About 3–6 hours |
| Intermediate-acting | About 2–4 hours | About 4–12 hours | About 12–18 hours |
| Long-acting | About 2 hours | No pronounced peak | About 24 hours |
| Ultra-long-acting | About 6 hours | No pronounced peak | About 36 hours or longer |

Premixed insulin combines different insulin preparations. According to the source, it generally begins working in about 15–60 minutes and may last about 10–16 hours; the peak depends on the specific mixture.

These timings are approximate and can vary by product and individual circumstances. The table is descriptive, not a dosing guide. Patients should follow the instructions for their prescribed insulin and confirm product-specific information with their healthcare professional or pharmacist.

## Ways to administer insulin

Insulin can be administered using different devices. The appropriate method depends on the prescribed treatment, practical needs, access, and patient preferences.

### Needle and syringe

Insulin can be drawn from a vial into a syringe and injected under the skin.

Common injection sites include the abdomen, thigh, buttocks, and upper arm. Absorption may differ by site. Repeated injections into exactly the same spot can cause changes in the underlying tissue and make injections less reliable.

Patients should receive instruction on injection technique and appropriate site rotation from a healthcare professional.

### Insulin pen

An insulin pen is a device used to administer insulin. Some pens are prefilled and disposable; others use replaceable insulin cartridges.

Pens may be easier for some people to handle than a vial and syringe. Certain models include dose-memory features or connected functions that record injection information.

Patients should learn how to use their specific pen and verify that the device and insulin product are compatible.

### Insulin pump

An insulin pump delivers insulin through a small device worn on or attached to the body. Some pumps use tubing, while patch pumps attach directly to the skin.

Pumps can deliver insulin continuously and may allow additional doses at mealtimes, according to the prescribed settings. Device features and the amount of automation vary by model.

Pump therapy requires training, monitoring, and a plan for device problems or interruptions in insulin delivery.

### Inhaled insulin

Some insulin products can be inhaled using a dedicated device. Inhaled insulin is an option for certain adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, but it is less commonly used than injectable insulin. Eligibility and safety considerations are product-specific and should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

### Automated insulin delivery systems

Automated insulin delivery systems combine a continuous glucose monitor, a control algorithm, and an insulin pump. The system uses glucose data to adjust insulin delivery automatically to varying degrees, depending on the device.

The NIDDK source describes artificial pancreas systems primarily in the context of type 1 diabetes. Patients with type 2 diabetes should not assume that every system is indicated or available for them; eligibility depends on the specific device, applicable authorization, and clinical circumstances.

## Side effects and medication safety

Diabetes medicines can cause side effects. Some medicines may cause hypoglycemia, particularly when their effects are not adequately balanced with food intake, physical activity, or other treatments.

Other possible side effects depend on the medication. Patients should ask their healthcare professional or pharmacist:
- Whether the medicine can cause low blood glucose.
- Which other side effects may occur.
- What to do if side effects develop.
- Whether food, alcohol, or other medicines may affect treatment.
- How the medicine should be stored.
- What to do if a dose is missed.
- Whether instructions change during illness or when unable to eat or drink normally.

Patients should follow the instructions provided for their specific medicine. Advice for one medication may not apply to another, so there is no universal rule for missed doses or medication use during illness.

Symptoms of possible hypoglycemia should be managed according to the patient's individualized treatment plan. Patients who experience repeated episodes or have difficulty managing medication side effects should contact their healthcare team.

## Questions to ask about a diabetes medicine

When starting a new medicine or reviewing an existing prescription, patients may find it useful to ask:

1. What are the generic and brand names of this medicine?
2. What does it do, and what is the intended benefit?
3. What dose should I take, and at what times?
4. Should I take it with food or on an empty stomach?
5. When should I expect it to start working?
6. Can it cause hypoglycemia or other side effects?
7. What should I do if I miss a dose?
8. What should I do if I am sick or cannot eat or drink normally?
9. Can it interact with other medicines, supplements, or alcohol?
10. How should I store it?
11. What should I do if I have difficulty affording or obtaining it?
12. Are there alternative treatments that may suit my needs?

Patients should keep an up-to-date list of their medicines and discuss any concerns about cost, access, side effects, or treatment burden with their healthcare team.

## Other treatment options

Some people with type 2 diabetes and obesity may be eligible for metabolic or bariatric surgery. This type of surgery can produce substantial weight loss and improve blood glucose management in selected patients. Some may experience diabetes remission or no longer need certain diabetes medicines for a period, but outcomes vary and remission is not guaranteed.

Decisions about surgery require specialist assessment, consideration of potential benefits and risks, and long-term follow-up. Surgery does not guarantee a permanent resolution of diabetes.

## Clinical research

Researchers continue to study diabetes medicines, insulin preparations, monitoring devices, and treatment delivery systems.

People interested in clinical trials should discuss potential participation with their healthcare professional. Trial eligibility, possible benefits, risks, and practical requirements vary. Participation in research should be an informed and voluntary decision.

## Source limitations and clinical use

This document adapts NIDDK educational material for people with type 2 diabetes. The original source also covers type 1 and gestational diabetes; those sections have been excluded or limited to context relevant to T2D.

Medication availability, indications, reimbursement, and device authorization can differ between the United States and other countries. The source is US-based and should not be treated as a complete guide to medication access or prescribing in other healthcare systems.

This document is for education and does not replace an individualized treatment plan. Medication choice, dosing, dose adjustment, and decisions about stopping or switching treatment must be made with an appropriate healthcare professional.
