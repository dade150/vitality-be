# Mental Health and Diabetes: Perspectives from People Living with Diabetes, Their Families and Carers

## 1. Psychological burden of diabetes

**Content type:** Organizational context  
**Population:** People living with diabetes, including people with type 2 diabetes  
**Topics:** Diabetes distress, emotional burden, self-management, psychological support

Diabetes requires ongoing self-management and can place a substantial psychological burden on people living with the condition and their families. Daily decisions concerning food, physical activity, medication and blood glucose management can make diabetes feel demanding and difficult to manage over time.

The continuous nature of diabetes management may contribute to emotional exhaustion, frustration and feelings of being overwhelmed. Diabetes-related psychological difficulties can include diabetes distress, diabetes burnout, depression, anxiety, eating-related difficulties and fear of hypoglycaemia. These experiences are distinct from one another and should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of a mental health diagnosis.

Psychological difficulties may affect quality of life, personal relationships and a person's perceived ability or willingness to manage their diabetes. Emotional wellbeing is therefore an important part of diabetes care throughout the course of the condition, rather than a concern to be addressed only at diagnosis or during a crisis.

People living with diabetes may benefit from timely, appropriate psychological support alongside their usual diabetes care. Support from family, friends, peers and healthcare professionals can also be important.

## 2. Personal testimonies from people living with type 2 diabetes

### 2.1 Erik: Acceptance and adjustment

**Content type:** Personal testimony  
**Diabetes type:** Type 2 diabetes  
**Country:** Netherlands  
**Topics:** Acceptance, adjustment, living with a chronic condition

Erik reports having lived with type 2 diabetes for 24 years. He describes his experience as sometimes difficult or frustrating, while also explaining that he has generally accepted his condition and learned to deal with it.

**Original testimony:**

> Sometimes living with type 2 diabetes is difficult or frustrating, but for me in general, I accepted my condition and deal with it.

**Psychological relevance:** This testimony illustrates one person's experience of accepting and adapting to a long-term condition. It should not be interpreted as evidence that acceptance follows the same course for everyone or that psychological distress is absent when a person has accepted their diagnosis.

### 2.2 Valerie: Stigma, the workplace and being heard

**Content type:** Personal testimony  
**Diabetes type:** Type 2 diabetes  
**Country:** Ireland  
**Topics:** Stigma, workplace challenges, fear of being misunderstood, patient-centred communication

Valerie highlights the difficulties that people living with diabetes may experience in the workplace. She describes how fear of appearing different or being misunderstood may lead some people to conceal their condition.

She also emphasizes that people living with diabetes develop substantial knowledge of their own bodies and experiences. In her view, healthcare professionals should devote more time to listening to patients during routine reviews.

**Original testimony:**

> I know that younger people living with diabetes can find the work environment a challenge. Some of them hide their diabetes for fear of seeming different or of being misunderstood.

> People living with diabetes are often experts about their own bodies and keeping them in balance. As such they should be listened to. HCPs should spend more time listening to patients as part of their regular review. For PwD, the prescription is only the beginning of the story.

**Psychological relevance:** This testimony highlights possible concerns about disclosure, social acceptance and workplace understanding. It also emphasizes the importance of listening to the patient's perspective and recognizing that treatment prescriptions alone do not capture the full experience of living with diabetes.

### 2.3 Ken: Depression, stress and social support

**Content type:** Personal testimony  
**Diabetes type:** Type 2 diabetes  
**Country:** United Kingdom  
**Topics:** Depression, stress, peer support, social support, non-judgemental listening

Ken describes personal experiences of depression and stress. He identifies support from friends and professional peer supervision as important sources of help in his own life.

He also emphasizes the importance of paying attention to changes in a person's demeanour, asking questions, listening without judgement and helping the person find appropriate support when needed.

**Original testimony:**

> I have suffered with mental health, with depression and stress. As a psychotherapist, I am lucky that I have my peer support supervisor and also relatively good help from friends. Friend support is vital.

> Be aware of what the person is not telling you, look at their demeanour and ask questions. Listen and don’t be judgemental. And if you cannot help them, make sure that you can direct or know of someone who can. Sometimes peer support groups can help as others maybe going through or have been through similar episodes.

**Psychological relevance:** This testimony illustrates the perceived value of supportive relationships, peer support and non-judgemental communication. It also highlights the potential importance of recognizing emotional difficulties and facilitating access to appropriate help.

**Interpretation note:** Ken's experience is a personal account. It does not establish the effectiveness of a particular intervention or imply that informal support can replace professional mental health care when needed.

## 3. Psychological support and person-centred diabetes care

**Content type:** Care recommendations and organizational perspective  
**Population:** People living with diabetes and their families or carers  
**Topics:** Integrated care, emotional needs, access to services, peer support, patient-centred care

The source emphasizes that diabetes care should address psychological and emotional needs alongside physical health and diabetes self-management.

Key themes include:

- **Recognition of psychological needs:** Emotional distress may accompany the demands of living with and managing diabetes. Psychological wellbeing should be considered as part of ongoing diabetes care.
- **Listening without judgement:** Healthcare professionals should create opportunities for people to describe their experiences, concerns and difficulties without fear of blame or stigma.
- **Individualized support:** People living with diabetes have different circumstances, coping strategies and support needs. Their own perspectives should inform discussions about care.
- **Social and peer support:** Family members, friends and peer communities may provide valuable emotional support and opportunities to share experiences.
- **Appropriate referral:** When a person's emotional needs exceed the support available in a particular setting, they should be helped to access an appropriate professional or service.
- **Accessible psychological care:** Psychological support should be available and affordable, with attention to barriers that may prevent people and families from obtaining help.
- **Integration of physical and psychological care:** Collaboration between diabetes care professionals and mental health professionals may help address the psychological dimensions of living with diabetes.

These themes reflect the source's advocacy for more responsive and integrated care. They should not be treated as a substitute for formal clinical guidelines or individualized professional assessment.

## 4. Key themes for retrieval

**Content type:** Thematic summary  
**Purpose:** Semantic retrieval and question answering

### Diabetes distress and emotional burden

The ongoing demands of diabetes self-management may contribute to frustration, emotional exhaustion and feeling overwhelmed. These experiences can affect quality of life and the perceived burden of daily self-care.

### Psychological adjustment and acceptance

People may develop different ways of adapting to type 2 diabetes. Acceptance is one possible personal experience, but individual responses vary.

### Stigma and fear of judgement

Negative stereotypes, blame and misunderstanding may contribute to emotional distress. Fear of discrimination or being treated differently may also influence whether a person discloses their condition in social or workplace settings.

### Depression and stress

People living with diabetes may experience depression or stress. Personal testimony can illustrate these experiences but cannot establish prevalence, causation or an individual diagnosis.

### Social and peer support

Supportive relationships may help people feel heard and less isolated. Peer groups can offer opportunities to connect with others who have faced similar experiences.

### Non-judgemental communication

Listening carefully, asking open questions and avoiding blame are recurring themes in the source. Recognizing a person's emotional concerns is part of understanding their experience of diabetes.

### Patient-centred and integrated care

Diabetes care involves more than prescribing treatment. The source emphasizes listening to patients, considering their emotional needs and facilitating access to appropriate psychological support.
