## Motivational Interviewing and Behaviour Change in Type 2 Diabetes: Purpose and scope

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative communication approach
that helps a person explore their own reasons for change. It can be
useful when discussing diabetes self-management, including physical
activity, eating habits, medication routines, and other agreed care
goals.

This document summarizes a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of
randomized controlled trials evaluating interventions that combined MI
with the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) in adults with type 2 diabetes
(T2D). The findings concern structured interventions, not MI as an
isolated technique.

## Core principles for patient-centred conversations

-   **Work collaboratively:** treat the person as an active partner in
    decisions rather than someone who must simply follow instructions.
-   **Explore the person's priorities:** ask what matters to them and
    which change, if any, feels realistic.
-   **Listen without judgement:** invite the person to describe
    difficulties, uncertainty, and previous experiences.
-   **Recognize ambivalence:** a person may want to improve their health
    while also finding change difficult. Explore both sides rather than
    arguing or blaming.
-   **Support autonomy:** offer information and options, and agree on
    goals together.
-   **Build self-efficacy:** identify existing strengths, past
    successes, available support, and small achievable next steps.
-   **Plan for barriers:** discuss practical obstacles and how the
    person might respond to them.
-   **Review and adapt:** revisit goals over time and adjust them to the
    person's circumstances and readiness.

MI is not a way to pressure, shame, or persuade a person into
compliance. It should support informed, voluntary decisions and be
adapted to the person's needs and preferences.

## The Transtheoretical Model: readiness for change

The Transtheoretical Model describes broad stages that can help tailor
conversations. These stages are a framework, not a diagnosis or a rigid
sequence. People may move back and forth between them, and readiness can
differ across behaviours.

### 1. Precontemplation

The person is not currently considering a particular change.

**Helpful approach:** ask permission to discuss the topic, explore what
the person already knows, and understand their priorities. Avoid
confrontation or assuming that they are unwilling.

### 2. Contemplation

The person is considering change but feels uncertain or sees both
benefits and drawbacks.

**Helpful approach:** explore ambivalence, ask what might improve if
they changed and what makes change difficult. Do not dismiss their
concerns.

### 3. Preparation

The person is interested in taking action and beginning to make plans.

**Helpful approach:** agree on a specific, realistic first step;
identify likely barriers; and decide what support may help.

### 4. Action

The person is actively trying the new behaviour.

**Helpful approach:** reinforce effort and progress, troubleshoot
difficulties, and adjust the plan collaboratively rather than treating
setbacks as failure.

### 5. Maintenance

The person is working to sustain a behaviour over time.

**Helpful approach:** identify what helps the behaviour continue,
anticipate changes in routine, and make a plan for lapses or difficult
periods.

## Communication techniques

The review describes MI as a person-centred approach. The following
commonly used techniques can help structure supportive conversations:

-   **Open questions:** invite more than a yes/no answer. For example:
    "What feels hardest about fitting activity into your week?"
-   **Affirmations:** acknowledge a person's effort, strengths, or
    persistence without exaggeration. For example: "You have already
    found one way to make your routine more manageable."
-   **Reflective listening:** summarize the meaning or feeling behind
    what the person says. For example: "You want to improve your health,
    but your work schedule leaves you exhausted."
-   **Summaries:** bring together the person's concerns, motivations,
    and ideas for a next step, then check whether the summary is
    accurate.

These techniques should be used naturally and respectfully, not as a
script. The person's own goals and preferences should guide the
conversation.

## Practical application to T2D self-management

A supportive conversation can focus on one behaviour at a time, such as
physical activity, food choices, medication-taking routines, glucose
monitoring when recommended, or attending follow-up appointments.

A practical sequence is:

1.  Ask which aspect of diabetes self-management the person would most
    like to work on, if any.
2.  Explore their current routine, priorities, confidence, and concerns.
3.  Discuss possible benefits and barriers from the person's
    perspective.
4.  Agree on a small, specific and realistic next step, or decide that
    more discussion is needed first.
5.  Identify support or resources that could make the step easier.
6.  Arrange to review what happened and adapt the plan without blame.

Goals should be individualized and consistent with the person's care
plan. This summary does not replace individualized advice from the
diabetes care team, especially for medication changes, glucose targets,
or exercise and dietary restrictions related to other health conditions.

## Findings from the systematic review and meta-analysis

The review included **30 randomized controlled trials with 4,214 adults
with T2D**. It examined interventions based on MI combined with the
Transtheoretical Model, compared with usual care or traditional diabetes
education.

**Interpretation:** across the included trials, the combined
interventions were associated with improvements in glycemic outcomes,
self-management, and self-efficacy compared with control conditions.
These are pooled group-level findings. They do not establish the effect
that a particular person will experience, and they should not be used to
promise a specific reduction in HbA1c or glucose.

## Intervention intensity: what the evidence does and does not show

The authors explored whether intervention characteristics helped explain
differences between studies. Their analyses suggested that the number of
sessions, session length, total intervention duration, and participants'
starting glucose levels may contribute to variation in outcomes. The
paper discussed patterns involving more than six sessions, sessions of
30 minutes or less, and total intervention time exceeding 90 minutes.

These findings are exploratory. They do **not** establish a universally
optimal schedule or a prescription that every person with T2D should
follow. The appropriate format depends on the person's needs, access to
care, preferences, and clinical context.

## Evidence limitations

Results should be interpreted with care:

-   The studies differed substantially in their interventions,
    participants, and outcomes, with high statistical heterogeneity for
    several outcomes.
-   The review judged 19 studies as having some concerns about risk of
    bias and 11 as having high risk of bias.
-   Only a minority of studies reported MI fidelity, limiting certainty
    about how consistently MI was delivered.
-   The findings apply to the structured, combined interventions
    studied. They do not isolate the effect of MI alone from the effect
    of the Transtheoretical Model or other elements of the intervention.
-   The review's inclusion and exclusion criteria limit how confidently
    the results can be generalized to people with clinical circumstances
    not represented in the included trials.

## Key points for patient-facing answers

-   Behaviour change can be difficult; uncertainty or setbacks should
    not be treated as a lack of effort.
-   A collaborative, non-judgemental conversation can help a person
    clarify their own reasons for change and choose a realistic next
    step.
-   The type of support can be adapted to how ready the person feels to
    change a particular behaviour.
-   Small, specific goals and follow-up may help make self-management
    plans more workable.
-   Research on structured TTM-based MI interventions in adults with T2D
    found improvements in several pooled outcomes, but study variability
    and bias concerns mean the results should be presented with
    appropriate caution.
-   Never infer an individual's motivation, readiness, or likely
    clinical response from these group-level findings.
