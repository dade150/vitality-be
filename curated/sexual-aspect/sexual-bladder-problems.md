# Diabetes, Sexual, and Bladder Problems

## Overview

Sexual and bladder problems are common with aging, but diabetes can contribute to their development or make them worse. People with diabetes may experience difficulties with sexual function or satisfaction, urinary leakage, frequent urination, urgency, or difficulty emptying the bladder.

Persistently high blood glucose can damage blood vessels and nerves. Diabetes-related nerve damage, also called diabetic neuropathy, may affect the genitals and urinary tract. Changes in blood flow, hormones, emotional health, and other medical conditions may also contribute to sexual difficulties.

Sexual and bladder symptoms should not automatically be attributed to diabetes. Assessment by a healthcare professional can help identify possible causes and appropriate treatment.

## When to seek medical advice

People with diabetes should speak with a healthcare professional about changes in sexual function, sexual desire, urination, or bladder control. These symptoms may indicate a complication or a need to reassess diabetes management.

Although discussing sexual or bladder symptoms may feel embarrassing, healthcare professionals are trained to address these concerns. Sexual health and bladder health can affect relationships, daily activities, and quality of life.

Prompt medical assessment is particularly important for painful or frequent urination that may indicate a urinary tract infection. According to NIDDK, bladder infections can progress to kidney infections and may worsen urinary leakage or retention.

## Risk factors

Factors associated with a greater likelihood of sexual or bladder problems in people with diabetes include:

- Blood glucose levels that are not well controlled.
- Diabetic neuropathy.
- Untreated high blood pressure.
- Untreated high cholesterol.
- Overweight or obesity.
- Physical inactivity.
- Certain medications.
- Excessive alcohol consumption.
- Smoking.

NIDDK also notes that genetic factors may influence susceptibility to diabetic neuropathy.

## Sexual health in men with diabetes

Changes in blood vessels, nerves, hormones, and emotional health may affect sexual function and fertility in men with diabetes.

### Erectile dysfunction

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is the inability to achieve or maintain an erection firm enough for satisfactory sexual intercourse.

NIDDK reports that more than half of men with diabetes develop ED and that men with diabetes are more than three times as likely to develop ED as men without diabetes. The source also notes that ED may occur 10–15 years earlier in men with diabetes.

Nerve damage and circulation problems may contribute to ED. Managing diabetes may help prevent or improve ED, and a healthcare professional can assess treatment options, including medication or changes to the diabetes care plan.

ED can have several causes. Clinical assessment is needed rather than assuming that diabetes is the only explanation.

### Retrograde ejaculation

Diabetes can sometimes cause retrograde ejaculation. In this condition, some or all of the semen enters the bladder instead of leaving the penis during ejaculation. The semen is subsequently passed out with urine.

Some men may experience little or no visible ejaculation. A healthcare professional can investigate suspected retrograde ejaculation, including through a urine sample collected after ejaculation.

Retrograde ejaculation may be relevant when a person is experiencing fertility difficulties.

### Peyronie's disease and penile curvature

Men with diabetes are more likely to have Peyronie's disease, a condition involving scar tissue, or plaque, in the penis. This can cause the penis to curve during an erection.

Penile curvature may make sexual intercourse difficult or painful. Some men with Peyronie's disease also experience erectile dysfunction.

### Low testosterone

Testosterone levels naturally decline with age, but abnormally low testosterone may contribute to erectile difficulties, reduced sexual desire, fatigue, or depressed mood.

Men with diabetes, particularly older men and those with overweight or obesity, are more likely to have low testosterone.

If low testosterone is suspected, a healthcare professional may recommend blood testing and a physical examination. Treatment may include prescription testosterone gel, injections, or patches in selected patients.

NIDDK notes that testosterone therapy may improve some sexual problems in men with diabetes, but it carries potential risks and is not appropriate for everyone. Treatment should be discussed with a healthcare professional rather than started without evaluation.

### Fertility problems

Some studies suggest that diabetes may affect sperm movement and the ability of sperm to fertilize an egg. Retrograde ejaculation can also interfere with conception.

Men who are having difficulty conceiving with a partner can seek assessment from a healthcare professional or fertility specialist. Depending on the cause, management may include changes to diabetes care, medical treatment, or specialist fertility procedures.

## Sexual health in women with diabetes

Women with diabetes may experience changes in sexual desire, arousal, genital sensation, vaginal lubrication, orgasm, or comfort during sex.

Potential contributing factors include:

- Nerve damage.
- Reduced blood flow to the genitals.
- Hormonal changes.
- High or low blood glucose levels.
- Fatigue, depression, or anxiety.
- Menopause and other health conditions.

A healthcare professional may recommend a physical examination, including a pelvic examination, and blood or urine tests to investigate symptoms.

### Reduced sexual desire and response

Sexual difficulties may include:

- Difficulty becoming or remaining aroused.
- Insufficient vaginal lubrication.
- Reduced genital sensation.
- Difficulty reaching orgasm or experiencing orgasm less frequently.

Both high and low blood glucose may affect sexual arousal. Fatigue, depression, anxiety, and other emotional or physical changes associated with living with diabetes may also reduce interest in sex.

Women who maintain blood glucose within their individualized target range may be less likely to develop nerve damage that can contribute to sexual difficulties. However, sexual symptoms may have multiple causes and may require specific assessment and treatment.

### Vaginal dryness and painful sex

Diabetes-related nerve damage may affect the signals involved in vaginal lubrication. Vaginal dryness can contribute to discomfort or pain during sexual intercourse.

A vaginal lubricant may help when dryness is contributing to discomfort. Persistent or painful symptoms should be discussed with a healthcare professional to assess possible causes and appropriate treatment.

Good long-term diabetes management may help reduce the risk of complications involving the nerves.

### Yeast infections

Women with diabetes may be more likely to develop vaginal yeast infections, particularly when blood glucose levels are elevated. These infections can cause discomfort or pain and interfere with sexual activity.

Symptoms of yeast infection can resemble those of other conditions, including sexually transmitted infections. NIDDK advises discussing symptoms with a healthcare professional rather than assuming the cause.

### Pregnancy and fertility

Women with diabetes who plan to become pregnant should discuss glucose management with their healthcare team before conception. High blood glucose during the early weeks of pregnancy can harm the developing baby, even before pregnancy is recognized.

Anyone with diabetes who is already pregnant should contact their healthcare professional promptly to establish an appropriate diabetes management plan.

Conditions associated with diabetes, including obesity and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), may contribute to fertility difficulties. A gynecologist or fertility specialist can assess persistent problems with conception.

## Bladder problems that can affect intimacy

Diabetes-related nerve damage can affect bladder function. Overweight and obesity may also contribute to urinary incontinence and other bladder symptoms.

Bladder symptoms are often treatable and should not simply be accepted as an unavoidable consequence of aging or diabetes. Depending on the symptoms, evaluation may include blood and urine tests or urodynamic testing.

### Frequent and urgent urination

Persistently high blood glucose can cause frequent urination. Some people with diabetes also experience a sudden, difficult-to-control urge to urinate, including at night.

Urinary urgency and frequency can interfere with daily activities and sexual intimacy. A healthcare professional can assess possible causes and treatment options.

### Difficulty emptying the bladder

Diabetic nerve damage may reduce awareness of bladder fullness or interfere with bladder muscle function. This can result in urinary retention, in which urine remains in the bladder after an attempt to empty it.

Urinary retention may contribute to urinary tract infections, leakage, and a persistent sensation of needing to urinate. Suspected retention should be assessed by a healthcare professional.

### Urinary incontinence

People with diabetes may experience different forms of urinary incontinence. Potential contributors include nerve damage, obesity, and bladder infections.

Urine leakage may cause embarrassment or lead people to avoid activities they previously enjoyed, including sexual activity. Management depends on the underlying cause. For people with overweight or obesity, weight management may help reduce leakage.

### Urinary tract infections

People with diabetes are more likely to develop urinary tract or bladder infections. Symptoms such as frequent, urgent, or painful urination warrant medical assessment.

Bladder infections can progress to kidney infections and may worsen existing bladder symptoms. Diabetes management is one part of reducing the risk of diabetes-related complications, but suspected infections require appropriate clinical evaluation and treatment.

## Prevention and management

Working with a healthcare team can help prevent or manage sexual and bladder problems associated with diabetes.

Strategies identified by NIDDK include:

- Keeping blood glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol within individualized target ranges.
- Being physically active.
- Maintaining a healthy weight.
- Stopping smoking.
- Seeking support for emotional or psychological difficulties.
- Discussing sexual or bladder symptoms with a healthcare professional.
- Reviewing the diabetes care plan when symptoms suggest that treatment may need adjustment.

Treatment depends on the problem and its underlying causes. Options may include medication, changes to diabetes management, lubricants for vaginal dryness, specialist evaluation, or psychological counseling.

People should not assume that improved glucose management alone will resolve every sexual difficulty. Other medical, hormonal, psychological, and relationship factors may need attention.

## Blood glucose and sexual activity

Sex is a form of physical activity. NIDDK advises people with diabetes, particularly those who use insulin, to consider checking blood glucose before and after sexual activity.

Both high and low blood glucose can cause problems during sex. The risk of hypoglycemia and the need for monitoring depend on the person's treatment regimen and individual diabetes management plan.

People who experience recurrent symptoms during or after sex should discuss them with their healthcare team. Any monitoring or treatment adjustments should be individualized rather than based on a universal rule.

## Psychological wellbeing and partner support

Changes in sexual function or desire can be distressing. Counseling may help people address concerns about sexual function, desire, or adjusting to health problems.

For people who have a partner, open communication may help them discuss concerns and seek support. When appropriate and desired by the patient, a partner may participate in a healthcare appointment or counseling session.

Sexual health is part of overall wellbeing. People with diabetes deserve respectful, nonjudgmental care for sexual and bladder concerns.

## Source limitations and clinical use

This document summarizes NIDDK patient guidance. It is intended for education and does not replace individual medical assessment.

The source covers diabetes generally and does not restrict all recommendations to type 2 diabetes. Its numerical estimates should be interpreted in the context of the original source and population rather than assumed to apply identically to every person with T2D.

Medication decisions, testosterone treatment, fertility procedures, and management of persistent sexual or urinary symptoms require assessment by an appropriate healthcare professional.
