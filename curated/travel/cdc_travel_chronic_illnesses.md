## Travelers with Chronic Illnesses: Purpose and key message

This document summarizes relevant guidance from the CDC Yellow Book 2026 chapter *Travelers with Chronic Illnesses*, with a focus on travel preparation for people living with diabetes.

Having a chronic condition does not usually mean that a person should not travel. The traveler and healthcare professionals should work together to ensure that the condition is appropriately managed and that medicines, supplies, and contingency plans are available throughout the trip.

## 1. Pre-travel consultation

The CDC recommends that travelers with chronic conditions arrange a pre-travel consultation ideally **4–6 weeks before departure**, and before paying for non-refundable travel where possible.

Preparation should involve the traveler, their primary healthcare professional, relevant specialists, and a travel medicine specialist when appropriate.

The consultation should consider:

- Destination, itinerary, duration, and mode of travel.
- Planned activities and time of year.
- The person's underlying health conditions and how travel might affect them.
- Previous health problems encountered during travel.
- Prevention strategies, vaccinations, medicines, and prescriptions relevant to the destination.
- Access to suitable healthcare at the destination.
- Airline or cruise-line requirements, including any need for special assistance or approval of medical equipment.

The itinerary may need to be adapted if the assessment identifies significant health or access-to-care concerns.

## Medication planning and medical supplies: Medication quantity and availability

The CDC recommends ensuring that travelers have enough medication for the entire trip, plus extra in case of unexpected delays.

- Carry medicines and essential medical supplies in hand luggage rather than checked baggage.
- Keep medicines in their original containers when possible.
- Bring relevant medical information and prescriptions.
- Discuss how and when to take medicines if the itinerary crosses time zones or changes normal meal schedules.
- Review possible side effects and interactions involving travel-related medicines or vaccines and the person's regular treatment.
- Identify pharmacies and healthcare professionals at the destination in case medicines or medical care are needed.

Any change to a medication or dosing schedule should be planned with the appropriate healthcare professional.

## Medication planning and medical supplies: Medication restrictions in the destination country

Before international travel, check whether the destination country restricts the importation of any prescribed medicines. Requirements vary by country and may apply even when a medicine has been prescribed legally in the traveler's home country.

The CDC source specifically recommends checking with the destination country's relevant embassy or consulate. Travelers should verify the current requirements for their particular medicines.

## Medical documentation and emergency planning: Medical letter

The CDC recommends obtaining a medical letter before travel. The letter should describe:

- Existing medical conditions.
- Prescribed medicines, including generic names and doses.
- Known allergies.
- Equipment required to manage the condition.
- The healthcare professional's name and contact information.

The source suggests obtaining this letter approximately **3–4 weeks before departure**.

Travelers may also consider storing relevant medical records and medication information in a secure, accessible digital format.

## Medical documentation and emergency planning: Medical identification

Consider wearing a medical alert bracelet or carrying medical information on one's person, particularly when traveling alone or when urgent treatment may be needed.

## Medical documentation and emergency planning: Healthcare access and insurance

Before departure:

- Identify healthcare professionals and pharmacies at the destination.
- Consider whether the planned destination and itinerary provide access to appropriate care if the person's condition worsens.
- Review travel insurance coverage, including medical treatment abroad and emergency medical evacuation.
- Check whether the airline or cruise line requires advance notice, medical documentation, or approval for medical equipment or assistance.

The CDC emphasizes that travelers should not assume their usual domestic health insurance will cover medical care abroad. Coverage depends on the policy and the country involved.

## 4. Travel preparation checklist

The following checklist reorganizes the CDC Yellow Book recommendations by approximate timing.

| Timing | Recommended actions |
|---|---|
| 4–6 weeks before travel | Arrange a medical and, when appropriate, travel medicine consultation. Review the destination, itinerary, planned activities, preventive measures, and access to suitable healthcare. Contact the airline or cruise line early if special assistance is needed. |
| 3–4 weeks before travel | Obtain a medical letter with diagnoses, medicines, doses, allergies, and required equipment. Check destination-country medication restrictions. Review insurance and emergency assistance arrangements. |
| 1–2 days before travel | Pack sufficient medication for the trip plus extra for possible delays. Keep medicines and supplies in carry-on baggage. Bring medical information and necessary supplies in their original containers when possible. |
| During travel | Stay hydrated when appropriate for the person's clinical needs, wear comfortable clothing, and walk or stretch at regular intervals during long journeys when feasible. Carry medical identification and seek medical care if the underlying condition worsens. |

This timeline is a planning aid. Some steps, such as checking medication restrictions or arranging medical equipment, may require more time depending on the destination and carrier.

## 5. Travel during long journeys

The CDC recommends staying hydrated, wearing loose-fitting clothing, and walking or stretching at regular intervals during long-distance air or ground travel when feasible.

People with additional health risks should discuss individualized precautions with their healthcare professional. This chapter includes specific considerations for conditions associated with a higher risk of complications during travel.

Seek medical attention if symptoms related to an underlying chronic condition worsen during the journey.

## 6. Diabetes-specific travel resource

The CDC Yellow Book's resource table identifies the following resources for travelers with diabetes:

| Organization | Resource |
|---|---|
| Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) | *Tips for Traveling With Diabetes* |
| American Diabetes Association (ADA) | *What Can I Bring With Me on the Plane* |

These resources complement the general preparation advice in this chapter. The CDC's *Tips for Traveling With Diabetes* provides practical advice on food, glucose monitoring, medicines, supplies, heat, and foot care.

## 7. Scope and clinical limitations

This chapter provides general guidance for people with chronic conditions traveling internationally. It is not a diabetes-specific treatment protocol and does not specify individualized medication adjustments.

For a person with type 2 diabetes, travel preparation should be adapted to their treatment regimen, risk of hypoglycemia, other health conditions, destination, and expected changes in meals or activity.

Use the person's established diabetes care plan and consult the treating healthcare professional when medication timing, doses, illness management, or fitness to travel is uncertain.
